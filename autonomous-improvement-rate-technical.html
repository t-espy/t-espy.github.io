Autonomous Improvement Rate — Technical Notes

A more precise treatment of the model, its limits, and what it predicts

← Read the short version

The hiring-facing version of the Autonomous Improvement Rate is intentionally simple. These notes make the assumptions explicit: what the terms mean, how they interact, how the model can fail, and what observations would count against it.

RA ∝ Et × IE × ηL

Improvement Rate ∝ Iteration Throughput × Information per Iteration × Learning Efficiency

Dimensional statement

The relation is proportional rather than a literal universal equality. A useful dimensional interpretation is:

  • Et — iterations · time−1
  • IE — information · iteration−1
  • ηL — Δperformance · information−1

Their product has units of Δperformance · time−1, which is what an improvement rate requires. Et × IE alone is an information-acquisition rate; ηL describes how effectively acquired information becomes progress against a declared objective.

The objective therefore has to be named before the model is measurable: test pass rate, held-out performance, yield, time-to-completion, out-of-sample risk-adjusted return, or another criterion declared before the search begins.

The factors are coupled

The three factors are not independent. Throughput purchased through parallelism can reduce the information available to later experiments because simultaneous iterations cannot condition on one another's results.

RA ∝ Et × IE(Et) × ηL

In practical terms, a batch of parallel experiments often contains redundant probes that a sequential process would have skipped after seeing earlier results. Parallelism can still be the correct trade — especially when iterations are cheap — but its throughput arrives with an information discount.

The engineering objective is therefore not to maximize Et, IE, or ηL independently. It is to find the operating point that maximizes their product under the system's real constraints.

Learning efficiency has two parts

ηL is easier to reason about if it is split into two mechanisms:

  • Retention: Does evidence survive into later iterations, sessions, and agents?
  • Exploitation: Does the retained evidence actually change the next action?

A system that repeatedly rediscovers the same fact has a retention problem. A system that remembers the fact but keeps making the same choice has an exploitation problem. Both reduce effective learning efficiency.

Verification fidelity determines direction

The most dangerous case is not ηL = 0. It is effective ηL < 0: the system is learning efficiently from a corrupted proxy for the real objective.

A weak test can reward incorrect software. A misleading simulator can reward a policy that fails in the real world. An optimization process can exploit noise in a backtest. In each case, more throughput and more efficient adaptation can accelerate movement in the wrong direction.

Verification fidelity therefore is not an implementation detail. It determines whether the measured improvement loop points toward the intended objective at all.

A cost-per-iteration prediction

The decomposition suggests a checkable prediction: as the cost of an iteration rises, the optimal allocation should shift from iteration throughput toward information gained per iteration.

Cheap, reversible domains can afford many attempts. Expensive physical experiments have stronger incentives to choose each experiment carefully. If iteration cost falls sharply in a domain, the optimal balance should move toward more exploration and higher throughput; if iteration cost rises, experimental design should become more valuable.

The constrained form

Real systems optimize under budgets. The useful question becomes: maximize RA subject to limits on money, compute, elapsed time, human attention, physical materials, or access to scarce models.

In my current software-development work, local inference is comparatively abundant while frontier-model access is the constrained resource. That makes Autonomous Completed Work per Frontier-Model Token a useful operating metric. It measures whether improvements to orchestration are actually converting scarce high-capability inference into more completed work rather than merely producing more agent activity.

Where the model is already useful

BuildLoop primarily attacks Et: repeated implementation, compile, test, diagnose, and repair cycles. Strategy Assayer is largely about fidelity: separating search fitness from credible out-of-sample evidence so optimization does not simply become faster overfitting. ADRs and agent-coord improve effective ηL by preserving decisions, findings, and ownership across agents and sessions.

The framework is useful here because it gives these otherwise separate engineering mechanisms a common purpose: increase the rate of trustworthy autonomous improvement rather than simply increasing activity.

Flywheel dynamics are a separate question

RA describes an improvement rate at a point in time. It does not, by itself, prove that improvement will accelerate indefinitely. The next useful improvement may be harder to discover than the previous one, and diminishing returns can overwhelm the positive feedback.

The recursive loop — better system → more autonomous work → faster system improvement → better system — is therefore a mechanism for feedback, not proof of runaway growth. Whether the loop accelerates, stabilizes, or slows is an empirical question.

What would count against the framework?

  • Systems with comparable Et, IE, and ηL persistently improving at very different rates without another explanatory factor.
  • Large changes in iteration cost producing no corresponding change in the optimal balance between throughput and experiment quality.
  • Measured improvement persistently outrunning the fidelity of the evaluation signal used to detect it.

The first case would suggest the decomposition is missing an important term. The latter two would challenge predictions derived from the framework rather than the dimensional decomposition itself.

Relationship to earlier work

None of the individual ideas starts here. Iterative improvement cycles long predate modern AI. Expected information gain comes from Bayesian experimental design and active learning. Throughput is a basic systems and operations concept. Knowledge retention and exploitation are central problems in learning systems and organizations.

The narrower claim is the multiplicative rate model: three named and separately instrumentable factors whose product describes autonomous improvement rate, generalized across software, computational research, simulation, robotics, and autonomous physical production — together with the throughput/information coupling, the fidelity condition on learning efficiency, and the cost-per-iteration allocation prediction.

Closest prior art

The closest antecedent I have identified is Lee et al., Toward self-driving laboratory 2.0 for chemistry and materials discovery, Materials Horizons (2026), DOI: 10.1039/D5MH01984B. That review describes self-driving laboratories as advancing along three interdependent axes: experiment throughput, information gained per experiment, and efficiency of converting data into scientific knowledge. AIR extends that decomposition beyond self-driving laboratories into a domain-independent model and develops the engineering consequences above.
